What is Design Research?

Design research is the process of gathering, analyzing and interpreting data and insights to inspire, guide and provide context for designs. It’s a research discipline that applies both quantitative and qualitative research methods to help make well-informed design decisions.

design researchdatainsightsquantitativequalitativedesign decisions

Design research is different from user experience research – focused on the usability of primarily digital products and experiences – design research is a broader discipline that informs the entire design process across various design fields. Beyond focusing solely on researching with users, design research can also explore aesthetics, cultural trends, historical context and more.

Source in lecture: Sanders, Liz. “An Evolving Map of Design Practice and Design Research.” ACM Interactions, vol. XV.6, Dec. 2008. Infographic.

Research Problem & Research Question

A research question is a clear and concise enquiry around which you center a research study. It helps to define the scope of the study and provides a basis for gathering and analysing a variety of data.

research questionclearconcise enquiryscopedata

A research problem addresses a specific issue, contradiction, or gap your study seeks to explore. It sets the scope and purpose. It guides direction and offers justification for the research.

Types of Research Questions

Qualitative — as stated in the lecture

These questions involve numerical data, measurement, and often lead to statistical analysis. Main purpose is to measure frequency, correlation, trends, or cause-effect relationships using numerical data.

numerical datameasurementstatistical analysisfrequencycorrelationtrendscause-effect

Q: Does the frequency of bus delays correlate with student tardiness to morning classes?

Q: How much time do students spend queuing in the canteen during peak hours?

Quantitative — as stated in the lecture

These explore experiences, behaviours, perceptions, and motives, usually through interviews, observations, or open-ended surveys. Main purpose is to gain deep understanding, uncover user pain points, and explore emotional or cognitive responses.

experiencesbehavioursperceptionsmotivesinterviewsobservationspain points

Q: How do first-year students perceive the usability of the university orientation app?

Q: What are the expectations of students regarding mental health support through digital tools?

Mixed Method

These combine both quantitative and qualitative elements to give a richer, more comprehensive understanding. Main purpose is to validate insights with data, while also understanding user perspectives and design implications.

mixed methodquantitativequalitativevalidate insightsuser perspectivesdesign implications
  • What is the impact of a real-time campus bus tracking app on student punctuality (quantitative), and how do students feel about using it (qualitative)?
  • How often do students use the digital noticeboard app (quantitative), and what improvements do they suggest for better communication (qualitative)?
  • What are the most used features of the campus food ordering app (quantitative), and how do users describe its convenience (qualitative)?

Qualitative Methods in Design Research

The lecture lists the following qualitative methods in design research: Ethnography; Observation – Covert & Overt; Interviews – Open & Closed; Shadowing; Cultural Probes & Diary Studies.

ethnographyobservationcovertovertinterviewsopenclosedshadowingcultural probesdiary studies

Qualitative methods capture user experiences, emotions, and motivations. They help understand “why” and “how”, including reasons and processes behind user actions, and provide rich, in-depth narratives that quantitative methods cannot capture.

user experiencesemotionsmotivationswhyhowuser actionsrich narratives

Example: Why students prefer studying in cafes vs. libraries.

examplestudent preferencescafeslibraries

Ethnography

Imagine a coffeehouse chain planning to expand its reach in a new city. Ethnographic research involves sending researchers to local neighbourhoods to immerse themselves in the coffee-drinking culture. They observe where residents go for coffee, how they socialize in coffee shops, and what flavours or rituals are popular. Armed with these insights, the chain can tailor its menu, ambience, and marketing strategies to resonate with local preferences and culture, ensuring a successful launch in the new market.

ethnographyimmersioncoffee culturesocializingritualslocal preferences

Ethnographic research is a qualitative method where researchers observe and/or interact with a study’s participants in their real-life environment. This type of research is popular in various fields like anthropology, sociology, and market research, among others, to understand cultures, behaviors, and social dynamics.

qualitative methodreal-life environmentanthropologysociologymarket researchculturesbehaviorssocial dynamics

Ethnographic research allows designers to understand their users' habits, mental models and behaviors deeply. For instance, a UX designer working on a travel booking platform might use ethnographic research to investigate how travelers plan and book their trips.

habitsmental modelsbehaviorsUX designtravel booking

Ethical Considerations in Ethnographic Research

One fundamental consideration in ethnographic research is privacy. Researchers must take steps to protect the privacy of their subjects.

privacyethicsparticipants

Obtain permission before taking photographs or recording conversations. Be careful not to reveal personal information about subjects that could lead to their identification.

permissionphotographsrecordingpersonal informationidentification

It’s also important to obtain informed consent from subjects before conducting any study activities. This means that people understand the study's purpose, what will be involved, and any potential risks or benefits. Ensure that any study does not cause harm or distress to subjects, either physically or emotionally. This may involve avoiding sensitive topics or situations that could trigger trauma.

informed consentstudy purposerisksbenefitsharmdistresssensitive topics

Interviews

Closed Questions

Closed questions (or closed-ended questions) restrict participants to one of a limited set of possible answers. Closed questions have a short and limited response.

closed questionslimited answersshort response

Examples of closed questions: What’s your job title? Have you used the website before? Approximately, how many times have you used the website? When was the last time you used the website?

job titlewebsite usefrequencylast use

Closed questions provide a short, clarifying response, while open-ended questions result in the user describing an experience.

clarifying responseopen-endedexperience
Open Ended Questions

Open-ended questions allow participants to give a free-form text answer. Open-ended questions encourage exploration of a topic; a participant can choose what to share and in how much detail. Participants are encouraged to give a reasoned response rather than a one-word answer or a short phrase.

open-endedfree-formexplorationdetailreasoned response

Examples of open-ended questions include: Walk me through a typical day. Tell me about the last time you used the website. What are you thinking? How did you feel about using the website to do this task?

typical dayexperiencethoughtsfeelings
Structured Interview

Questions are predetermined and set in a particular order. Typically, the questions will be closed-ended and will offer multiple choices. Since the questions are asked in a set order, it is easy for the researcher to compare responses and determine the patterns. Structured interviews help to mitigate biases and offer higher levels of validity and reliability.

structuredpredeterminedset orderclosed-endedcompare responsespatternsbiasvalidityreliability

This type of interview is used when you understand the subject thoroughly and can design excellent questions; you don’t have sufficient time and resources to carry out the research; or your research question depends on a strong parity between the respondents.

subject knowledgetimeresourcesparityrespondents
Semi-Structured Interview

Semi-structured interviews have elements of both structured and unstructured interviews. With these interviews, the researcher will have an idea of the questions to ask, but the phrasing and order of the questions will not be set. Since they are often open-ended, the researcher will enjoy high levels of flexibility, and they will still be able to compare the responses fairly easily.

semi-structuredstructuredunstructuredflexibilityopen-endedcompare responses

These types of interviews are ideal when the researcher has a lot of experience in interviews or the research question is exploratory in nature.

experienceexploratoryresearch question
Unstructured Interview

Also known as non-directive interviewing, and they don’t have a set pattern. Also, questions are not arranged in advance. These interviews are used as exploratory research tools and are commonly used in social sciences and humanities.

unstructurednon-directiveno set patternexploratorysocial scienceshumanities

An unstructured interview is a good fit if the research question is completely exploratory in nature or if the research requires you to form a connection with your respondents.

exploratoryconnectionrespondents
Focus Group

With focus group interviews, the researcher will present the questions to a group instead of an individual. These types of interviews don’t just study the responses of the interviewees; they also study the group dynamic and body language. Focus groups give us an opportunity to capture the human element – the emotions and non-verbal cues that numbers often miss – and help us to explore underlying motivations.

focus groupgroupgroup dynamicsbody languageemotionsnon-verbal cuesmotivations

Focus group interviews can be suitable if the study depends on group discussion dynamics, if the questions are complex and can’t be answered with multiple choices, or if the study is open to uncovering new ideas and questions.

discussion dynamicscomplex questionsnew ideasnew questions
Interview comparison shown in the lecture

Structured interviews are less susceptible to interviewer bias, offer high levels of credibility and validity, and are simple to use, but can make interviewees uncomfortable, are not flexible, and have limited scope because they are closed-ended. Unstructured interviews are highly flexible, respondents are more open as it is a casual conversation, have lower risk of bias, and offer more details and nuance, but can be hard to generalize, can be hard for the interviewer to keep opinions or feelings in check, and may risk low internal validity.

structuredbiascredibilityvalidityflexibilitygeneralizationnuance

Semi-structured interviews limit distraction during the interview, combine the benefits of structured and unstructured interviews, and offer high levels of detail. The lecture also notes flexibility, potential validity concerns, a high risk of bias, and the need to come up with good questions for these interviews.

semi-structureddetailflexibilityvaliditybiasgood questions

Focus groups are highly effective, interviewees will be more open, can be done on a low budget, and make it easy to discuss a variety of topics. The lecture notes possible disadvantages including the interviewer not being able to ask too many questions, requiring excellent leadership and social skills, risk of social desirability bias, and inability to guarantee confidentiality.

Observation

The lecture presents observation through two distinctions: covert versus overt observation, and participant versus non-participant observation.

observationcovertovertparticipantnon-participant

Overt observation means the observed group is aware of the presence of the researcher and that their behavior is being observed.

overtawareresearcherobserved behavior

Covert observation means the participants are not aware of the presence of the researcher and that their behavior is being observed.

covertnot awareresearcherobserved behavior

Participant observation: the observer becomes actively involved in the activities of the people being studied.

participantactive involvementactivities

Non-participant observation: the researcher observes from a distance.

non-participantdistanceresearcher

The lecture comparison notes that overt observation has no ethical issues but behaviour may not be natural/normal; covert observation has high ecological validity but ethical issues; participant observation can make behaviour easier to understand and has high ecological validity but is hard to record retrospectively and can affect objectivity; non-participant observation records observations as they happen and allows the observer to maintain objectivity, but behaviour may be recorded without knowing the meaning behind it.

ethical issuesecological validitynatural behaviourobjectivityretrospective recordingmeaning

Shadowing

Shadowing in UX research is a method of observing users as they perform their regular tasks or activities, in order to gain insight into their behaviors, needs, and pain points. The researcher, just like a private investigator, follows the user closely, either in person or remotely, and takes notes on their actions.

shadowingregular tasksbehaviorsneedspain pointsin personremotely

Although the process of user shadowing includes asking questions, it is different from in-depth interviews and field (or ethnographic) research: observation must be prioritized, and questions only support it.

observationquestionsin-depth interviewsethnographic research
Hawthorne Effect

The Hawthorne effect is a phenomenon when people modify their behavior because they know they are being observed, which typically results in the performance improvement.

Hawthorne effectobservedbehavior modificationperformance improvement

Diary Study

Diary study—is a longitudinal (running over a period of time) UX research method used to collect qualitative data, through participants keeping a diary to record their thoughts, feelings, and behavior, while they use a product.

diary studylongitudinalqualitative datathoughtsfeelingsbehavior

A diary study entails participants self-reporting data over an extended period of time—ranging from days to a month or longer. During this time, they’ll log specific information about activities regarding the product being studied.

self-reportingextended perioddaysmonth or longeractivitiesproduct

The lecture timeline illustrates planning and preparation, a pre-study brief, a reporting period, payment milestones, a post-study interview, and data analysis.

Cultural Probe

Cultural probes are an approach to qualitative user research where face-to-face research is impractical or inappropriate. They consist of prompts, questions and instructions along with artifacts for recording thoughts and feelings. The artifacts may be as simple as a diary or as elaborate as a single-use camera.

cultural probesqualitative researchface-to-facepromptsquestionsinstructionsartifactsthoughtsfeelings

Cultural probes are specifically casual and informal, yet thoughtful in their aesthetic craft, message, and delivery, created to inspire delight and respect, response and return. The materials created for probe kits should be varied and imaginative, designed to elicit responses that are relevant to the particular design inquiry.

casualinformalaesthetic craftdelightrespectvariedimaginativedesign inquiry
